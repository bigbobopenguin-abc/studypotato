Topic 1.1Manufacturing vs Production

In plain English

Manufacturing is making the thing. Production is everything needed to get enough of it to customers: planning, buying, making, checking, packing and delivering.

Remember
  • Manufacturing transforms raw materials into finished or semi-finished products by physical, chemical or mechanical processes
  • Production is broader: planning, procurement, manufacturing, assembly, inspection, packaging, storage and delivery
  • Production systems: job (unit), batch, mass, continuous (process)
Think of it as
A bakery: baking the bread is manufacturing. Ordering flour, scheduling bakers, checking quality, bagging and delivering to shops is production.
In the test
  • Define and differentiate manufacturing and production
  • Name and match the 4 production systems to examples
Manufacturing

Transforming raw materials or components into finished or semi-finished products using physical, chemical or mechanical processes.

e.g. CNC machining an aluminium block, casting an engine housing, forming a car panel, 3D printing a plastic part.

Production

The broader process of creating goods or services to meet customer or market demand.

Planning · material procurement · manufacturing · assembly · inspection · packaging · storage · delivery.

Asks
Manufacturing“How do we make the product?”
Production“How do we produce the required quantity efficiently and deliver it to the customer?”
Key relationship

Manufacturing is one part of production. Production also covers planning, buying materials, inspection, packaging, storage and delivery.

Types of production systems (by volume)

SystemVolumeExample
Job (unit) productionOne-off or very small quantities, often customCustom prosthetic hand, a bridge, a prototype
Batch productionGroups of identical items, then switch to the next batchBakery, a run of 500 brackets
Mass productionVery large quantities of standard itemsBottle caps, cars, phone casings
Continuous (process) productionNon-stop flow, 24/7Oil refining, chemicals, steel, paper

Topic 1.2The Six Main Manufacturing Process Categories

In plain English

There are six families of ways to make things: pour it (casting), inject it (molding), bend or squash it (forming), cut it (machining), connect it (joining), or build it layer by layer (additive).

Remember
  • Casting: molten material poured into a mold (sand, die, investment)
  • Molding: thermoplastic injected under high pressure; very low unit cost only at high volume
  • Forming: deforms solid material, no material removed (forging, extrusion, rolling)
  • Machining: subtractive, removes material; high accuracy (±0.01 mm) but creates swarf
  • Joining: temporary or permanent joints (welding, soldering, brazing, adhesives, fasteners)
  • Additive: layer by layer from a 3D model; geometric freedom, no tooling, slow and costly at volume
Think of it as
Making a shape out of clay: pour it into a mould, press it into a mould, squeeze it into shape, carve it from a block, glue pieces together, or stack thin layers.
In the test
  • Describe each process with an example
  • Give one advantage and one limitation of each
  • Contrast forming vs machining and casting vs molding

Manufacturing processes = methods of converting raw materials into finished products.

Pairs the notes contrast
  • Casting and molding share a principle: a cavity defines the shape. Efficient at scale, but consider melting energy, mold life, scrap and recyclability.
  • Forming prioritises productivity and material efficiency; machining prioritises precision and flexibility.
  • Additive manufacturing does not replace traditional manufacturing. It expands the options: prototypes, personalised products, complex low-volume parts, aerospace and medical.

Topic 1.3–1.6Factors Affecting Process Selection

In plain English

Choosing a process means checking it against what the product needs: material, shape, quantity, quality, cost, time, equipment, skills, sustainability and flexibility.

Remember
  • 10 factors: material, geometry, volume, quality, cost, time, equipment, labour, sustainability, flexibility
  • Low volume favours low tooling cost; high volume justifies dedicated tooling
  • Mass production: repeatability, cost efficiency, quality control; lean = less waste
Think of it as
Choosing transport for a trip: a plane is fast but expensive, a bus is cheap but slow, a car is flexible. The right choice depends on the trip.

Topic 2Manufacturing Process Selection Matrix

In plain English

The selection matrix turns “which process?” into numbers: give each criterion a weight, rate each process 1–5, multiply, add up, and the highest total wins. Then justify it.

Remember
  • Weighted score = weight × rating; total = sum of weighted scores
  • Weights add up to 1.00; ratings 1 (poor) to 5 (excellent)
  • Highest total is preferred, but always check the trade-offs
  • Framework: define requirements → identify candidates → evaluate → select → justify
Think of it as
Rating phones before buying: battery matters most to you (big weight), the camera a bit (small weight). Score each phone, multiply by importance, add up.
In the test
  • Build a weighted matrix and calculate totals
  • Choose weights that suit the product (medical vs consumer)
  • Write recommendation, key trade-off and final decision

A decision-making tool to systematically compare processes and pick the most suitable one for a specific product, moving from “naming a process” to a structured, evidence-based decision.

CriterionMeaning
Production volumeSuitability for low, medium or mass production
Unit costExpected manufacturing cost per product
Dimensional accuracyAbility to achieve the required dimensions and tolerances
Material compatibilitySuitability of the process for the required material
Surface finishQuality and appearance of the finished product
Tooling lead timeTime to prepare tools, molds or equipment
How the matrix works
Weighted score = Weight × Rating
Total score = sum of weighted scores for that process

Weights show how important each criterion is for this product and must add up to 1.00. Ratings are 1–5: 1 = poor / very unsuitable, 2 = below average, 3 = average, 4 = good, 5 = excellent / highly suitable. The highest total is generally preferred, but still check the trade-offs (tooling cost, volume, quality, material, sustainability, lead time).

The 5-step process selection framework

  1. Define product requirements: material, volume, tolerance, surface finish, application, regulations, geometry, budget
  2. Identify candidate processes (and select criteria with weights)
  3. Evaluate alternatives: rate each process 1–5 per criterion
  4. Select the optimal process: highest total score
  5. Justify the decision: recommendation, key trade-off, final decision
Weights change with the product

For a medical device, dimensional accuracy and material compatibility get the biggest weights; unit cost matters less, because low volume and high value justify a higher cost. For a high-volume consumer product, unit cost and volume suitability dominate.

Topic 3.1Product Life Cycle (PLC)

In plain English

Every product's sales follow a story: launch (introduction), take-off (growth), plateau (maturity) and fade (decline). Each stage needs different business and manufacturing decisions.

Remember
  • PLC is a market model: introduction → growth → maturity → decline
  • Introduction: low sales, flexible small-batch production
  • Growth: sales rise fast, increase capacity
  • Maturity: sales high but slowing, cut unit cost and differentiate
  • Decline: sales fall; redesign, reposition, replace or discontinue
Think of it as
A hit song: few people know it at first, then it goes viral, then everyone has heard it, then it fades as new songs come out.
In the test
  • Name the 4 stages with sales pattern and focus
  • Match a scenario to its stage
  • State the manufacturing priority for each stage

PLC describes the stages a product passes through in the marketplace, from introduction to eventual decline and withdrawal. It shows how sales, demand, competition and profit change over time.

The four stages
Introduction → Growth → Maturity → Decline
StageSales patternBusiness focusManufacturing priorityWater bottle example
IntroductionLow sales; awareness developing; profit low or negativeBuild awareness, get feedback, test market demandFlexible production, prototypes, small quantities, find problems earlyNew bottle with a temperature display; few buyers; collect reviews
GrowthSales rise rapidly; competition increasesIncrease capacity, keep quality, strengthen market positionIncrease capacity, improve planning and supplier coordination, standardise, cut defectsBuy more equipment, order more stainless steel and seals
MaturitySales high but growth slows; market saturatedStay competitive, differentiate, protect profitReduce unit cost, improve productivity, minimise defects and scrapNew colours and sizes, better insulation, less waste
DeclineSales fall (new tech, changing tastes)Decide: redesign, reposition, replace or discontinueReduce output, avoid overproduction, control inventorySmart bottles take over; reduce production, clear stock
Watch for
  • PLC is a market model. Its stage doesn't automatically decide the manufacturing method; that also depends on design, volume, quality and cost.
  • Some products stay in maturity for years; a product's life can be extended by redesign, new features or new markets.

Topic 3.2Product Lifecycle Management (PLM)

In plain English

PLM keeps everyone working from the same, correct, up-to-date product information, from first idea to end of life, and controls how that information changes.

Remember
  • PLM manages product information, processes and collaboration across the lifecycle
  • Key concepts: CAD, BOM, revision control, ECO, documents
  • PLM stages: conception → design → production & launch → service → end-of-life
  • PDM manages product data; PLM adds processes and collaboration (PDM is part of PLM)
  • PLM is not just a CAD system
Think of it as
A shared cloud document with version history and approvals, instead of everyone emailing their own copy of a drawing.
In the test
  • Define PLM and list its key concepts
  • Name the 5 PLM stages
  • Differentiate PLM and PDM

PLM is a systematic approach to managing product-related information, processes and collaboration throughout the product lifecycle, from concept and design to manufacturing, service and end-of-life. It ensures everyone uses accurate, consistent and traceable information.

Typical activities: CAD design, BOM management, revision control, engineering change orders, manufacturing coordination.

Why PLM matters: every department needs different information

DepartmentInformation needed
Design & engineeringCAD models, drawings, product specifications
ManufacturingProcess plans, machine settings, assembly instructions
PurchasingMaterial specifications, supplier details, component requirements
Quality assuranceInspection criteria, test results, defect records
Sales & marketingProduct features, approved specs, customer requirements
Service & maintenanceProduct configuration, replacement parts, repair instructions

Without PLM, departments may use outdated drawings, order the wrong components, or make parts that don't match the approved design.

Key PLM concepts

  • CAD: models and drawings define geometry, dimensions and technical requirements.
  • BOM (Bill of Materials): lists the components, parts and materials needed to make or assemble a product.
  • Revision control: records changes so users can identify the correct approved version.
  • ECO (Engineering Change Order): documents, reviews and approves a change before it is implemented.
  • Documents: drawings, specifications, technical reports, manufacturing instructions.

The 5 PLM stages (engineering lens)

StageMain focus
1. Conception & ideationIdentify customer needs, generate ideas, define requirements
2. Design & developmentDevelop designs, select materials and components, test prototypes
3. Production & launchManufacture the product and introduce it to the market
4. Service & maintenanceSupport, repair, maintenance and updates
5. End-of-life managementReuse, refurbishment, recycling or responsible disposal

The sequence is not strictly one-way: service data can trigger a redesign, and end-of-life findings can shape the next concept.

PLM vs PDM

PDM (Product Data Management)PLM (Product Lifecycle Management)
FocusProduct dataThe whole product lifecycle
ScopeProduct information and its controlsInformation + processes + collaboration
Typical functionsCAD files, BOMs, documents, revision controlPDM + ECO workflows, approvals, cross-department coordination
ObjectiveAccurate, controlled product dataManage product information and activities across the lifecycle
Example · the cap design changes
PDM: controls the updated CAD model, drawings, BOM and revision history. PLM: also runs the ECO, gets approvals, tells manufacturing and purchasing, and manages the knock-on effects on production and product support.

Benefits of PLM

Improved quality (approved info) · fewer errors (no outdated drawings) · better collaboration · improved efficiency · better traceability (what changed, why, which versions).

Common trap

PLM is not just a CAD system. CAD creates models and drawings; PLM manages product information and the workflows around it. PDM is usually part of PLM.

Topic 3.3–3.4Life Cycle Management (LCM) and Comparing PLC, PLM, LCM

In plain English

LCM looks at a product's whole life (materials, making, shipping, use, disposal) and asks how to reduce its environmental, economic and social impacts.

Remember
  • LCM manages environmental, economic and social impacts across the whole life cycle
  • Stages: raw materials → manufacturing → packaging & distribution → use → end-of-life
  • Less material isn't automatically more sustainable if the product fails sooner
  • PLC = market, PLM = information & changes, LCM = whole-life impacts
Think of it as
Judging a car not just by its price, but by fuel use, repairs and resale value over its whole life.
In the test
  • Name the 5 life-cycle stages with a sustainability action each
  • Name the 3 sustainability dimensions
  • Compare PLC, PLM and LCM

LCM manages a product's environmental, economic and social impacts across its whole life cycle, from raw material extraction to end-of-life. It looks beyond manufacturing cost or immediate performance.

Typical activities: material selection, energy and resource efficiency, Life-Cycle Assessment (LCA), waste reduction, reuse and recycling. Example: is a recycled-aluminium bottle lower-impact overall than a plastic one?

3. Packaging & distributionPackaging use, protection, transport emissionsRecyclable cardboard inserts, consolidated deliveries
4. Product useDurability, maintenance, service lifeReplaceable silicone sealing ring
5. End-of-lifeRepair, reuse, disassembly, recycling, responsible disposalDesign for easy disassembly; separate steel body from plastic cap

Three dimensions of sustainability

DimensionConsidersWater bottle example
EnvironmentalResources, energy, emissions, wasteLess material waste, better recyclability

Key idea

Using recycled material or less material isn't automatically sustainable. If the bottle breaks easily and must be replaced often, it can be worse overall. Judge across the whole life cycle.

Topic 4PLM Functions and Product Data Workflow

In plain English

PLM software organises product data around six building blocks: the Part, its Documents, the BOM it belongs to, its Revision, its Cost and the ECOs that change it.

Remember
  • Part identifies the item; Document describes it; BOM defines structure; Revision identifies version; Costing evaluates cost; ECO controls change
  • Single-level BOM: no subassemblies. Multi-level BOM: nested subassemblies
  • Cost hierarchy: Target < Estimated < Calculated < Actual; the highest set base overrides
  • An ECO doesn't mean approval; a revision is the resulting version
Think of it as
A recipe book: each ingredient is a Part, the instructions are Documents, the ingredient list is the BOM, “version 2 with less sugar” is a Revision, the shopping bill is Costing, and asking the head chef to approve a change is an ECO.
In the test
  • Explain each of the 6 concepts
  • Read a single- and multi-level BOM and roll up costs
  • Apply the cost hierarchy and the 7 ECO steps

PLM organises product data and keeps the relationships between it, so authorised users get the right information. Why companies need it: accurate data, traceability, collaboration across departments, controlled engineering changes.

#ConceptAnswersIn one line
1PartWhat is the product/component?Identifies the engineering item
2DocumentWhat information describes it?Provides information about the item
3BOMWhat is it made of?Defines the product structure
4RevisionWhich version are we using?Identifies the applicable version
5CostingHow much does it cost?Supports cost evaluation
6ECOWhat needs to change?Controls review, approval and implementation of changes

Workflow: Part → Document → BOM → Revision → Costing → ECO.

1 · Part

  • An identifiable engineering item: component, manufactured item or whole assembly (brackets, shafts, gears, housings, fasteners).
  • Record contains a part number, name, description; linked to documents, revisions, BOM and cost.
  • Classification types: Material (used to make a component) · Component (used to make an assembly) · Manufacturing (used during production) · Assembly (used to make a larger assembly or finished product).
  • Assigned Creator = the owner, responsible for designing the part (usually an engineer); may differ from whoever typed it in. Designated User / Reviewer = reviews the part before it's used or released.
Part ≠ CAD file

The Part identifies the engineering item; the CAD file is a digital representation of its geometry. The part number tells apart components that look alike.

2 · Document

Information that describes, specifies or supports a product: drawings, specifications, design reports, inspection reports, manufacturing instructions, maintenance manuals. Linked to a Part (e.g. a bracket's drawing for dimensions and tolerances, plus a material specification). Managed with ID, revision history, lifecycle status and access permissions.

3 · Bill of Materials (BOM)

  • A structured list of all parts, components, subassemblies and materials needed to make a product, showing parent → child relationships.
  • Hierarchical: finished product at the top, then subassemblies, then components. Includes part numbers, descriptions, quantities, revisions (and possibly costs, drawing references).
  • Links design with purchasing, production planning and manufacturing. Errors cause shortages, assembly problems, higher costs and delays, so update the BOM after every approved change.
Single-level BOM

Simple products with no subassemblies: one level of parts, listed in part-number order.

Multi-level BOM

Complex products: subassemblies broken down into further subassemblies. Every item links to a parent (except the top level).

Example · bracket assembly
1 × bracket @ $2.50 = $2.50 4 × screw @ $0.10 = $0.40 4 × washer @ $0.05 = $0.20 4 × nut @ $0.08 = $0.32 Unit cost of the assembly (rolled up) = $3.42

4 · Revision

  • Manages and records changes to designs, drawings, specs and documents. Each revision (A, B, C…) is one version of a controlled definition.
  • Records changes, dates, responsible people and approval status. Previous revisions are kept to preserve history.
Part number vs revision

The part number identifies the item; the revision identifies which version of its definition. Bracket BRK-1001 Rev A → Rev B is the same part, updated.

5 · Costing

Cost typeMeaning
TargetThe desired cost level to achieve
EstimatedForecast cost from available information and judgement
CalculatedSystem-generated: sum of the children's Estimated and/or Actual values
ActualThe cost actually incurred or recorded
UnitRolled-up cost of one assembly based on its BOM
Cost base hierarchy (low → high)
Target → Estimated → Calculated → Actual

If several are set, the highest base overrides the lower ones: Calculated overrides Estimated and Target; Actual overrides all. A parent's cost is acquired automatically from its BOM.

6 · Engineering Change Order (ECO)

A formal record that controls an engineering change through review, approval and implementation. Triggers: customer feedback, design improvements, quality problems, supplier changes, manufacturing difficulties, cost reduction, new requirements.

  1. Identify the problem or improvement opportunity
  2. Describe the proposed change
  3. Identify affected Parts, Documents and BOMs
  4. Evaluate technical, cost, quality and production impacts
  5. Review and approve
  6. Update affected records and revisions
  7. Implement and maintain traceability
ECO vs revision

The ECO controls the change process; a revision identifies the resulting version. Creating an ECO does not mean the change is approved.

How the six connect

Changing one component can affect its documents, the assembly BOM, revision information and the rolled-up unit cost. The ECO makes sure all of these are evaluated and updated. PLM connects engineering information, product structure, cost and change control; it isn't just file storage.

Written · 8

1. Differentiate manufacturing and production, with one example each.
Manufacturing transforms raw materials into products using physical, chemical or mechanical processes (e.g. casting an engine housing). Production is broader: everything needed to meet demand, including planning, procurement, manufacturing, assembly, inspection, packaging, storage and delivery (e.g. planning and delivering 10,000 water bottles a month).
2. Compare forming and machining for a steel bracket made at 100,000 units/year.
Forming (stamping and bending) suits high volume: good material utilisation, high productivity and low unit cost once tooling is paid. Machining gives better accuracy (±0.01 mm) and flexibility but creates swarf and has a higher unit cost and longer cycle times. For 100,000 brackets with moderate tolerances, choose forming; use machining only for features that need tight tolerances.
3. Weights: volume 0.4, cost 0.3, accuracy 0.3. Process A rates 5, 4, 3; process B rates 3, 3, 5. Which wins?
A = 0.4×5 + 0.3×4 + 0.3×3 = 2.0 + 1.2 + 0.9 = 4.1. B = 0.4×3 + 0.3×3 + 0.3×5 = 1.2 + 0.9 + 1.5 = 3.6. A wins, but still state the trade-off (B is more accurate).
4. List the 5 steps of the process selection framework.
Define product requirements → identify candidate processes → evaluate alternatives → select the optimal process → justify the decision.
5. A new smart bottle has just launched. State its PLC stage and two manufacturing priorities.
Introduction stage. Use flexible production with small quantities while demand is uncertain, and find design and manufacturing problems early using customer feedback.
6. Explain the difference between PLC, PLM and LCM using a water bottle.
PLC: how the bottle performs in the market (e.g. deciding whether to discontinue a declining model). PLM: managing its information and changes (e.g. controlling a revised cap design with an ECO). LCM: its whole-life impacts (e.g. comparing recycled aluminium vs plastic over the full life cycle).
7. Give one sustainability action for each of the 5 LCM stages of a water bottle.
Raw materials: recycled stainless steel. Manufacturing: reduce cutting offcuts and defects. Packaging and distribution: recyclable cardboard, consolidated deliveries. Use: replaceable sealing ring for a longer life. End-of-life: design for disassembly so steel and plastic can be separated and recycled.
8. The screw supplier changes. Describe how PLM handles this using the six concepts.
Raise an ECO describing the change; identify the affected Part (screw), Documents (specification), and the BOMs that use it; evaluate technical, cost and quality impact (Costing: the rolled-up unit cost changes); review and approve; issue a new Revision of the affected records; implement and keep traceability.
